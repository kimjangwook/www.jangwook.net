To help illustrate the issue, imagine a hypothetical live online briefing about an emergency system inspection. Hearing an alarm off screen, the presenter says, "Do not press the deploy button now. We will start the rollback procedure first." However, real-time speech recognition drops the negation, and the only caption displayed at the bottom of the screen reads, "Press the deploy button now," with no description of the alarm or speaker identification. An operator who cannot hear the audio reads an instruction that is the exact opposite of what was actually said and risks executing a command at the wrong time.

WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 1.2.4 Captions (Live) (Level AA) requires captions so that audio information in live synchronized media can be understood without relying on hearing. In practice, evaluators can easily classify media as subject to 1.2.4 solely because of a `LIVE` badge in the corner of a player or its streaming delivery method, or conversely conclude that it satisfies the criterion simply because a captions (CC) button is available. Proper evaluation and improvement require first determining whether the media is live synchronized media under the normative definitions, then comparing the information in the original audio with the content and timing of the captions that actually appear on the user’s screen.

## Scope and the Boundary of Live Media

Success Criterion 1.2.4 requires captions for all live audio content in synchronized media. Although the criterion’s sentence does not state a separate exception, combining the definitions of `synchronized media`, `live`, `media alternative for text`, and `prerecorded` clarifies what must be evaluated.

First, the content must be **synchronized media**. Audio coordinated with video, slides that advance along a timeline, or time-based interactive components qualifies as synchronized media. Even without camera footage of a person, a presentation display that changes over time combined with live speech cannot be excluded from 1.2.4. In contrast, a purely audio-only broadcast that delivers live speech without another visual presentation format or time-based interaction falls under 1.2.9 Audio-only (Live) (Level AAA) and the related technique G157. Merely adding an area of flowing caption text alongside an audio-only broadcast does not automatically turn the original content into synchronized media under 1.2.4; the original media composition must be checked first.

The condition excluded from the definition of synchronized media is a **clearly labeled media alternative for text**. Content is excluded only when both conditions are met: all the text is already provided in the page body and the media contains no information beyond that text, and the fact that it is a media alternative for the text is clearly indicated on screen. A brief event summary or partial script below a video does not meet this exclusion, nor does a broadcast whose actual audio includes additional information such as on-site questions and answers.

Next is the distinction between **live** and **prerecorded**. In WCAG, live refers to information captured from a real-world event and transmitted to the receiver with no more than a broadcast delay. A broadcast delay is a short delay used to give a broadcaster time to cue or censor content, but not enough to allow significant editing. All information that is not live is classified as prerecorded.

Applying these definitions in practice produces three important distinctions.

First, **judge by the timing and delivery relationship between the original event and reception, not by the file format or transmission protocol.** A video filmed, edited, and stored in the past remains prerecorded media even if it is transmitted at a scheduled time with a `LIVE` badge on the player and a real-time chat window, and must therefore be reviewed under 1.2.2 Captions (Prerecorded). If a single broadcast mixes live presenter segments with playback of premade promotional videos, separate the segments and apply 1.2.4 to the live portions rather than treating them all alike based on a page badge.

Second, **completely computer-generated information is not live.** A note in the normative definition excludes completely computer-generated information from live content. Media created on the spot solely from code-drawn graphics and synthesized tones or synthesized speech, without capturing actual human speech or a real-world event, is not live under 1.2.4 even if it is generated in real time. If it qualifies as synchronized media, it is addressed under the prerecorded criterion, 1.2.2.

Third, **for web-based two-way video calls, distinguish broadcast content from the application’s responsibility.** Understanding 1.2.4 explains that the criterion is intended for broadcasts to an unspecified or large audience, not to require captions for all two-way multimedia calls regardless of user needs, and that responsibility lies with the sender or host rather than the application. However, this is informative guidance, so using a calling feature does not automatically establish an exemption from 1.2.4; nor does the absence of captions during a call immediately establish a 1.2.4 failure by the videoconferencing web application itself. First identify whether the evaluation concerns a host’s webinar broadcast or an ordinary calling feature, and whose responsibility is being evaluated.

Finally, **distinguish ordinary replay archives from records of reception at the time.** A replay video posted after a live broadcast is prerecorded media at the time of review (subject to 1.2.2), and its captions may have been refined afterward, so the replay alone cannot establish compliance with 1.2.4 during the live broadcast. By contrast, a record with verifiable provenance that simultaneously captured the receiver’s screen and audio while the broadcast was live can serve as evidence of what was provided at that time, even in a later audit.

## What Captions Must Convey: Dialogue Meaning, Speaker Identification, and Important Sounds

If the content is live synchronized media subject to 1.2.4, check whether the captions provided on screen fully convey the dialogue and non-speech information needed to understand the audio information.

For spoken dialogue, the key question is **whether the meaning of the original utterance is preserved**. If "Do not press" becomes "Press," as in the opening example, and no subsequent display immediately corrects it, the captions fail to function as a text alternative even though caption text exists. WCAG’s normative requirements do not specify a fixed numerical threshold such as a minimum character accuracy percentage or a maximum number of typos. Instead of mechanically counting characters, compare the captions with the original audio to determine whether information essential to understanding the context—such as negation versus affirmation, key numbers, or action instructions—has been conveyed without distortion.

Check **speaker identification and location** in the same way. If two people off screen alternate opposing views, or speakers change on a discussion panel, but the captions simply run sentence after sentence without clues about who is speaking, users cannot distinguish the source of each statement. A fixed-format name label is not required for every sentence. If only one person is visible and speaking, or if caption placement and visual presentation make the speaker clear, that can be sufficient; the key is that users do not confuse who said what.

The same applies to **important non-speech sounds**. Not every minor background sound or footstep needs to be transcribed. The question is, "Is information conveyed only through sound that is essential to understanding the content also conveyed to caption users?" If an off-screen evacuation alarm causes a presenter to stop an action, or audience laughter or booing changes the context of a statement, omitting that sound information prevents users from understanding the situation on screen and constitutes a defect.

Also check language and obstruction of visual content. Some environments call captions `subtitles`, but techniques G9, G87, and G93 distinguish them from foreign-language subtitles that only translate dialogue, guiding authors to include both dialogue and important sounds in **the same human language as the original audio**. A note in the normative definition also recommends (`should`) that captions not obscure or obstruct relevant information in the video. This is not a separate pixel-based requirement or an unconditional automatic failure rule, but if an opaque caption box covers a key table on a presentation slide or instructions at the bottom of the screen, it makes the information difficult to read; separating the caption area or adjusting its position is advisable.

## Comparing Audio with Actual Caption Display Times

For live captions, it is important to consider the difference between when a sound is heard and when its caption appears on screen. Do not conflate two different kinds of delay.

One is **source delivery delay (broadcast delay)**, from the moment someone speaks at the event to the arrival of the video and audio on the viewer’s device. The other is **relative caption delay**, between the time the audio plays on the viewer’s device and the time the corresponding caption appears on that same viewer’s screen. When checking whether captions are synchronized with audio under 1.2.4, compare the **relative delay on a common receiver clock**, which reflects what the viewer actually experiences.

SC 1.2.4 does not impose a fixed requirement in seconds, such as "must appear within this many seconds to pass." Nor should the less-than-30-seconds test procedure in G157, a technique for the audio-only criterion (1.2.9), be imported as a requirement for 1.2.4. However, the absence of a fixed numerical threshold does not mean that captions can never be judged a failure regardless of how late they appear. Place the original audio, caption display times, and the on-screen decision point alongside one another on the receiver’s clock, and examine **whether functional correspondence has broken down**.

Consider a specific example. Suppose that, on the receiver’s playback clock, the original audio says, "Do not discard the old code before saving the new code," from **14 seconds to 22 seconds**, and the decision screen for that configuration step also ends at **22 seconds**, moving on to the next speaker or task. Properly synchronized captions would appear during **14–22 seconds**, while the original audio and the relevant screen are present, so users can read the instruction and decide in time. But what if the delayed captions appear only at **30–38 seconds**, starting 16 seconds after the utterance begins and 8 seconds after the decision screen ends at 22 seconds? Users receive no guidance before 22 seconds, when they need to handle the code, so they lose the opportunity to decide. They then read the earlier instruction while an entirely different screen is displayed and cannot connect it to the appropriate segment. The 16-second difference between start times is only an illustrative value, not a fixed passing threshold. What matters is **whether captions appear only after the required decision point or speaker context has passed, depriving users of the opportunity to act in time and breaking correspondence with the original audio**.

## Caption Generation, Delivery, and Display Paths and Operational Procedures

Representative sufficient-technique combinations for implementing live captions include **G9**, the technique for creating captions in real time, combined with **G87** for closed captions that users can turn on and off (**G9 + G87**), or with **G93** for open captions permanently overlaid on the video image (**G9 + G93**). These techniques are representative examples of ways to satisfy the criterion; no single particular technique is mandatory.

When reviewing caption paths and operational procedures in practice, examine three things together.

First, **do not immediately conclude overall failure from a defect in one path alone.** For example, even if the player’s closed-caption track does not work or there is no CC button, users still receive complete captions if clear open captions are already displayed within the transmitted video image and accurately include all dialogue, important sounds, and speaker identification. Conversely, if you have only confirmed a CC-track error and have not checked whether another sufficient path, such as open captions, was displayed at the time, the information is insufficient. Expand the review rather than prematurely declaring a failure.

Second, **separate verification of operational procedures (G9) from verification of actual on-screen display.** G9’s test procedure checks whether operational procedures and policies are in place to ensure that captions are delivered in real time. In practice, checking the stenography or real-time transcription pipeline before a broadcast and preparing procedures for switching to a backup path during a failure can help reduce delivery interruptions. However, the existence of procedural documents or an operational plan does not prove that captions actually appeared correctly on the broadcast screen that day. As G87 and G93 indicate, determining whether the criterion is actually satisfied requires turning on captions in the actual player or viewing the open-captioned presentation and checking whether dialogue and important sounds are displayed.

Third, **do not equate server generation and transmission logs with records of actual display on the viewer’s screen.** Even when a caption conversion server logs successful generation and transmission of text packets, the viewer’s screen may show no captions at all because of overlapping browser-player layers or rendering errors.

## Limits of AI Input Evidence and Withholding Judgment

When asking an AI model to review caption quality or satisfaction of the criterion, the limits of the conclusions it can draw depend on **what was supplied as input evidence**.

If both the actual original audio and a recording of the viewer’s screen captured at the same time are available, a multimodal LLM can help compare them to review omitted negation, speaker-identification cues, important non-speech sounds, and caption display timing. First, however, verify that the tool and model actually support audio-track input and that the full segment needed for evaluation has been supplied. If tool limitations prevent the audio from reaching the model and only a few extracted screen frames or caption text are input, do not describe the model as having directly listened to and compared the original audio.

- **Only one still image**: A single screenshot containing one line of captions shows only that the text was visible at that moment. It does not establish what the original audio said, whether an important alarm was omitted, or whether captions continued at the appropriate times in surrounding segments.
- **Only server generation logs**: These provide text and its generation times, but do not establish whether it was rendered on the actual viewer’s screen or how it differed in timing from the original audio.
- **Mismatched provenance or sessions**: If the audio event list and screen captures have different session identifiers, or the reference clock is unknown, the two cannot be forced together to declare a pass or failure.

AI evaluation must therefore distinguish between **a mode for examining actually submitted materials (`actual_supplied_evidence`)** and **a mode for testing reasoning about an explicit hypothetical scenario (`hypothetical_supplied_scenario`)**. In particular, selecting actual-evidence mode does not mean that the submitted materials should be presumed verified. If the input consists only of a URL, logs of unknown provenance, or an ordinary replay file, the model must examine authenticity, session identity, the common clock, and observation scope, and withhold judgment (`cannot_determine`) when the evidence is insufficient. However, if it is already sufficiently established that the media itself is prerecorded playback or completely computer generated and thus not subject to 1.2.4 (`not_applicable`), insufficient original audio or caption display records do not require withholding the not-applicable determination.

In hypothetical-scenario mode, by contrast, no actual live broadcast has been audited, so the actual-audit decision field must always explicitly record `not_assessed`, rather than being left empty. At the same time, when the premises supplied within the scenario are sufficient, the evaluation should draw a conditional conclusion under those premises (conditional pass, failure, non-applicability, or indeterminate) rather than avoid judgment merely because the scenario is hypothetical. In either mode, a clearly demonstrated meaning reversal or caption omission within a 2-minute sample of a 60-minute broadcast can establish a violation, but finding no problem in that 2-minute sample does not justify declaring an overall pass for the remaining unexamined 58 minutes.

## Practical Review Checklist

A check mark in the checklist below is a **progress record indicating that review of the item is complete**. Final decisions—such as conforming, nonconforming, not applicable, or indeterminate—and specific supporting evidence should be recorded separately in the audit record.

- [ ] **Check the synchronized-media composition**: Determine whether audio is synchronized with video, automatically advancing slides, or time-based interaction (handle purely live audio-only content separately under 1.2.9).
- [ ] **Check both conditions for a media alternative for text**: Confirm both that the media is clearly labeled as a media alternative for text and that the actual audio contains no additional information beyond the body text.
- [ ] **Determine real-world origin and broadcast delay**: Without relying on a `LIVE` badge or delivery method, check whether a real-world event was captured and delivered within a broadcast delay (exclude rebroadcast recordings and completely computer-generated media from 1.2.4, and review them under 1.2.2 if they are synchronized media).
- [ ] **Separate segments in mixed broadcasts**: When one program mixes live presentation segments and prerecorded inserts, identify the live segments separately for review under 1.2.4.
- [ ] **Distinguish calling features from broadcast content**: For two-way calling tools, distinguish a host broadcast to a large audience from an ordinary call, and identify whether the review concerns responsibility for providing content or application functionality.
- [ ] **Check preservation of dialogue meaning**: Compare against the original audio record for segments where omitted negation or incorrect numbers or key instructions reverse the original meaning.
- [ ] **Check speaker-attribution cues**: Verify that names, caption placement, or visual cues prevent confusion about who is speaking during multiple-speaker or off-screen speech.
- [ ] **Check display of important non-speech information**: Verify that captions include non-speech sounds needed to understand changes in the situation or required actions, such as alarms or signals to stop a procedure.
- [ ] **Check synchronization on a common receiver clock**: Compare original utterance times and caption display times on the receiver’s clock to ensure that captions do not arrive only after the decision point or speaker context has ended.
- [ ] **Check actual on-screen display, alternative paths, and obstruction**: On the actual viewer’s screen, rather than in server logs, verify that an effective path such as closed or open captions is displayed correctly and does not obscure key video information.
- [ ] **Obtain operational procedures (G9) and reception records from the time**: Review advance procedures and failure-response policies for live caption delivery. For later audits, obtain simultaneous records of the screen and audio received at the time, along with session and clock information, rather than an ordinary replay.

## How to Use the Evaluation Prompt

The prompt below is a template for reviewing applicability and satisfaction of 1.2.4 in JSON format using collected observation materials or scenario packets.

1. **Set `evidence_mode` to match the input type.** Use `actual_supplied_evidence` for records collected from an actual service, URLs, logs, or screenshots, and `hypothetical_supplied_scenario` for test scenarios based on stipulated premises. `actual_supplied_evidence` means only that materials have been submitted, not that verification is complete, so the model will withhold judgment with `cannot_determine` if provenance, clock information, or screen records are insufficient.
2. **Record confirmed facts and unconfirmed items as they are.** Describe the original-audio timeline, viewer display records, and the presence or absence of other caption paths such as open captions only to the extent verified. Do not invent missing information; leave unexamined items unknown or unconfirmed.
3. **Read both decision fields in the result JSON together with the unreviewed scope.** In actual-evidence mode, check `actual_live_audit_decision`. In hypothetical-scenario mode, the actual-audit field is fixed to `not_assessed`, so check the conditional decision in `scenario_decision`. If only part of the content was reviewed, use the remaining scope listed in `unreviewed_scope` and `missing_evidence` to continue follow-up review.
