## 2. What English readers in 2026 actually flag

**Corpus study, not a 2023 list.** *The Economist*, “How to spot AI writing” (30 Jul 2026): [economist.com/culture/2026/07/30/how-to-spot-ai-writing](https://www.economist.com/culture/2026/07/30/how-to-spot-ai-writing). Method, as reported by [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91584243/how-to-identify-ai-generated-writing-viral-report-has-surprising-new-clues-economist) (3 Aug 2026) and [Dataconomy](https://dataconomy.com/2026/08/04/how-to-spot-ai-writing/) (4 Aug 2026): 55,940 sentences / 1.2 million words; ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok vs *Economist* / *NYT* / *Washington Post* plus novels 1950–2022.

Derek Thompson’s digest of that piece: [x.com/DKThomp/status/2084271141477113926](https://x.com/DKThomp/status/2084271141477113926), **~610k views, 3,587 likes, 2,819 bookmarks** (3 Aug 2026).

What the study (via those recaps + Thompson) says **is** a 2026 tell:

- Long sentences with **too little** punctuation; fewer commas, semicolons, parentheses than humans.
- **“and”** as the most overused word.
- Lists of three (feeds the “and” count).
- Polysyllabic adjectives: “significant,” “increasingly.”
- Decorative scientific jargon: “rate-limiting,” “parameter.”
- Nominalizations (expansion, implementation) where a verb would do.
- “It’s not X, it’s Y.”
- Verbosity; blocky paragraphs; **limited sentence-length variation**.
- Clearest current signs, in the report’s own wording as Dataconomy quotes it: **verbosity + light punctuation**.

What the same study says **is no longer** a tell: em dashes. Only Claude used more than humans; ChatGPT now uses fewer than any model tested and fewer than humans.

**A working editor, not a stylometry paper.** Jon Severs (editor, *TES*), 4 Feb 2026: [x.com/jon_severs/status/2018990290791739796](https://x.com/jon_severs/status/2018990290791739796), **~1.66M views, 17k likes**. Claim: “Even the worst human writing has an abundance of signals about the writer in the choices they make. AI-written pieces give no signals.” Features he names in the thread:

- Endless front-loading of sentences.
- Repeated “Because of X, Y” shifts.
- Ubiquitous bullet points.
- Phrasing that mirrors templated political speeches.
- Pacing that is “overly robotic (short; long; short; long) and unrelated to the point.”
- A call-to-action climax.

Reply in-thread (Siam, 695 likes / 55k views on that reply): people who use em dashes now rewrite to avoid looking like slop — the same false tell the Economist later measured.

**Reader-scale tells on X, Aug 2026 (smaller, but same list):**

- Eva ([x.com/ParanormalJunk2/status/2087684560226255084](https://x.com/ParanormalJunk2/status/2087684560226255084), 73 likes / ~3.4k views): “quietly”; “and here’s what people miss”; “it’s not X, it’s Y”; writing that “tries to be profound, but actually makes no sense.”
- Independent restatement, same week: “Binary contrasts… Faux-insight setups… Fake-profound endings (‘The future isn’t coming. It’s already here.’)” ([x.com/gunzify0/status/2087598048327176222](https://x.com/gunzify0/status/2087598048327176222)).
- Marie Drouvin ([x.com/circecreates/status/2087985865788211303](https://x.com/circecreates/status/2087985865788211303)): she now “define[s] things primarily by what they are rather [than] what they are not” and “just start[s] with Y.”

**Why the contrast form exploded.** Eryk Salvaggio, “It’s Not Just X. It’s Y.” (31 May 2026): [mail.cyberneticforests.com/its-not-just-data-its-post-training](https://mail.cyberneticforests.com/its-not-just-data-its-post-training). He names the construction (negative parallelism), tracks it on LinkedIn, and argues RLVR-style “reasoning” training rewards “it wasn’t Thursday, it was Wednesday” traces. He also records Grammarly flagging his own human phrase “automated language production” as 11× more likely AI and “align with” as 43× — so detector-driven rewriting is now a tell of its own.

**HN applying the 2026 list to a technical essay.** Ivan Turković, “AI Made Writing Code Easier. It Made Being an Engineer Harder” (25 Feb 2026), [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47206824): **403 points, 310 comments**. **What Severs’s “no signals” looks like in a human piece that worked:** Huang’s 2 mm connector and Bedwars screenshot; Kalam’s addendum that he later contracted for Hack Club; Goedecke linking a resume and saying “10 years… two big-but-not-quite-FAANG companies”; Henley quoting the pip chapter and refusing the AI mandate. ## 3. Unknown term, stakes, opening — named examples

**Unknown term — function, then name, after a reason to care.**

Julia Evans, PATH (13 Feb 2025). She does not open with a definition of `PATH`. She opens with a conversation and a failed search: instructions said “add this to `~/.bashrc`,” but “what if you’re not using bash?” The term is introduced as the thing that makes `http-server: command not found` go away. Shell is identified by a command (`ps -p $$`) and by what it prints, not by a taxonomy. Bash’s three config files are settled by an `echo hi there` experiment, not a flowchart (she links one and says not to internalize it). “Gotcha” sections are named as failures the reader will have (`it ran the wrong program`), not as concepts.

Goedecke, “Seeing like a software company.” “Legible” is not defined first. Scott’s German forest (inspectable rows, dead underbrush, less wood) runs for a section; then: “It’s the same way in software companies.” Only after that: “By ‘legible’, I mean work that is predictable, well-estimated, has a paper trail…” Enterprise deals arrive as the *reason* the company pays for Jira, not as a vocabulary lesson.

Lynch’s Docker rewrite in [Rules for Writing Software Tutorials](https://refactoringenglish.com/excerpts/rules-for-software-tutorials/) (2 Jan 2025; [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42574641) **376 points / 74 comments**). Bad open: abstractions. Good open: “Do you have a production server that you’re terrified to touch…?” Then: “Docker is a tool for packaging your app so that it has a consistent, reproducible environment wherever it runs.” Problem → job → name.

Lynch’s rule for the same book: “Avoid jargon, abbreviations, or terms that would be meaningless to a newcomer.” A knowledgeable reader can skip; a newcomer cannot decode “JSX transpilation.”

**Stakes — a number, a deadline, a named loser.**

Kalam: 11 years of history, $5k → $50k this week + $200k/year, workspace deactivated, “Dozens of our staff and volunteers… scrambling.” The reader does not need a Slack architecture diagram.

Goedecke, “How I ship”: default state of a project is “not ship”; an uncelebrated launch “is not a ship”; trust with leadership outranks a bug-free deadline. Stakes are career-visible (review time, who gets the next project), not abstract “quality.”

Huang: “Making this laptop was hard. Mentally pressured with a deadline, and a constant inter-disciplinary challenge.” Concrete: <0.5 mm between keyboard and heatsink; audio DAC abandoned for time.

Henley: $5,000 advance, 12–15% royalties, median sales “single thousands of copies,” a chapter count and illustration quota in the contract. Stakes are time and voice, stated in publisher units.

**Opening paragraph — title + first three sentences decide.**

Lynch (Mar 2025, 603 HN points): on arrival the reader asks (1) did you write this for someone like me, (2) how do I benefit. “Give yourself the title plus your first three sentences.” His own sample:

> **if got, want: A Simple Way to Write Better Go Tests**  
> There’s an excellent Go testing pattern that too few people know. I can teach it to you in 30 seconds.

Lynch on Bob Nystrom’s *Crafting Interpreters* intro ([19 Nov 2025](https://refactoringenglish.com/blog/crafting-interpreters-intro/)): four sentences do four jobs — welcome, say what the book is, say what else it is, say why this author. Contrast he quotes from the Dragon Book: “Programming languages are notations for describing computations to people and to machines.” He reads that as “for computer science theorists and not regular developers like me.”

Goedecke, “How I ship,” paragraph 1: years, role, claim, split (shipping ≠ writing code). No history of Agile.

Kalam: after the resolution update, the origin story is three facts (11 years, $5k, then the threat). No “In today’s SaaS landscape.”

Evans, PATH: a friend, a search that failed, three questions the existing docs don’t answer. Table of contents immediately after, so a skimmer can leave.

Huang: Tesla quote, then a bold spec sentence, then video. The first original sentence is a parts list a non-EE can parse (Minecraft, battery hours, open-source).

## 4. English register, from these pieces and these readers

**Contractions.** Evans, Goedecke, Kalam, Huang, Henley, Nystrom all mix `I’m` / `don’t` / `won’t` with uncontracted forms in the same section (`I do not think`, `I cannot`). None of the high-reach 2025–26 pieces are contraction-free, and none are contracted in every clause. The Economist 2026 study does not list contraction rate as a tell. Severs does not either. Uniform “always contract” and uniform “never contract” are both undocumented as 2026 reader tests; mixed density is what the named human pieces actually do.

**First person.** Default in every piece that traveled. Evans: “I was talking to a friend… I actually couldn’t find.” Goedecke: “I have shipped a lot… I’m good at it.” Kalam: we/I with a later conflict-of-interest addendum. Huang: “I first made a mental map.” Nystrom: “I’m really excited we’re going on this journey together” — Lynch’s point is that this sentence is the anti-Dragon-Book signal, not fluff. Severs’s complaint is the *absence* of a chooser, not the presence of “I.”

**Hedging.** Goedecke, on-writing: he will write “companies reward pragmatic engineers,” not “in my experience, I’ve seen companies reward pragmatic engineers, but every company operates differently.” His substitute for per-sentence hedges is a public resume and concrete scope (“10 years… two… companies”). He says zero caveats sound “dictatorial”; all-caveats “buries your actual point.” HN’s 1,425-point thread on “How I ship” is people arguing the claim, which is the load he says he wants. Salvaggio’s 2026 note is the other side: RLVR-style writing *over-performs* contrastive certainty (“it wasn’t X, it was Y”) and squeezes out “the openness to doubt.”

**Sentence-length variance.** Two documented failure modes, not one:

1. Economist 2026: AI runs **long**, joins with **and**, uses **few** internal marks, little length variation, blocky paragraphs.
2. Severs: AI also fakes variation as a **metronome** — short; long; short; long — “unrelated to the point being made.”

Human pieces above change length when the thought changes: Goedecke’s short “Have courage!” after a long production paragraph; Evans’s one-line “It worked!” after a command block; Kalam’s “That’s absurd.” after a long Salesforce sentence. Parentheses and asides appear in Goedecke and Evans at a rate the Economist says models under-use.

**When a heading should be a sentence.**

- **Claim heading (sentence or near-sentence)** when the section *is* the argument: Goedecke “Shipping is hard,” “What is shipping?,” “Can we ship right now?” Lynch: if a skimmer saw only headings, they should still see “the bones of a good story.” His $46k-redesign post beat a tutorial whose headings “don’t reveal much.”
- **Action heading (sentence-case, often a clause)** when the section is a procedure: Evans `step 1: what shell are you using?`, `problem 1: it ran the wrong program`. Lynch’s tutorial rules: consistent point of view and tense; “Install Go 1.23” over “Why Choose Go?”
- **Noun phrase** when the heading names an object or borrowed frame, not a move: Goedecke “Seeing like a state,” Huang “Powertrain,” Evans “a note on bash’s config file.”
- **Don’t** use heading-shaped voids (“Overview,” “Conclusion,” “The bigger picture”) or a final heading that is a call to action (Severs’s “call to action climax”).

**Bullet density.** Severs flags “ubiquitous bullet points” as an AI feature. Goedecke uses short lists inside sections that already argued in prose, then a summary list. Evans uses lists for mutually exclusive shells. Kalam barely uses them. The human pattern in these pieces: bullets after a claim, not instead of one.

## 5. Lines for an English writing prompt

1. Title plus the first three sentences must answer two questions: is this for a reader like me, and what do I get if I stay. If you are in paragraph two without answering, cut the opening.
2. Open on a situation with a number, a deadline, or a failed attempt. Do not open on a definition, a landscape (“in today’s X”), or “this post will.”
3. When a term may be new, show the job it does in the scene first, then name it. One extra sentence that swaps a specialist word for a word a neighboring reader already has (wallet, not Merkle tree) is the usual cost of a wider audience.
4. Stakes are countable consequences for named people (money, deleted history, who gets the next project, a 0.5 mm gap). Do not state stakes as importance.
5. Hold one opinion a competent reader might reject. Do not hedge it in every sentence. Put the scope of your experience once, in concrete units (years, companies, what you actually shipped).
6. Write in first person. Mix contractions with uncontracted clauses. Do not make every sentence casual, and do not make every sentence formal.
7. If a section argues, the heading is the claim (“Shipping is hard”). If it is a procedure, the heading is the reader’s next action (“step 1: what shell are you using?”). Noun-phrase headings only name objects. A skimmer who reads headings and looks at figures should be able to reconstruct the piece.
8. Let sentence length follow the thought. Do not build long sentences on repeated “and” with almost no commas, semicolons, or parentheses. Do not alternate short/long/short/long as a cadence. Use asides when a human would.
9. Do not write “it’s not X, it’s Y.” Do not default to lists of three. Do not use “quietly,” “here’s what people miss,” “the future isn’t coming, it’s already here,” or a front-loaded “Because of X, Y.” Do not pad with “significant,” “increasingly,” or a noun made from a verb if the verb would do. Do not sprinkle scientific jargon that is not load-bearing.
10. Do not strip em dashes to look human; in 2026 they are not a reliable machine mark, and only some models overuse them.
11. Leave at least one irregular, sourced detail only this writer would have (a measurement, a refusal, a screenshot, a conflict-of-interest line). Generic competence with no chooser is what editors are now calling AI.
12. Do not announce the document, recap a scene you just showed, or end on a moral or a call to action. End when the last fact has done its work.
